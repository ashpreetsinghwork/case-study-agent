## Framing and disclosed constraints

The author states up front this is a solo, two-week capstone project for a
design masterclass, that the problem statement was handed to him rather than
self-discovered, and that no problem validation was done — all disclosed
plainly before the narrative starts, rather than left for the reader to
notice.

## Definitions

Before describing the problem, the piece defines its own vocabulary:
"Products" (independently purchasable offerings), "Features" (things bundled
within a product suite), High-Intent Users (organic, already know what they
want) and Low-Intent Users (exploratory traffic, often from ads).

## The problem

Razorpay offers roughly 20–25 products spanning payments and neo-banking.
The author's diagnosis: these products are categorized along overlapping
dimensions at once (by brand, by application type, by how new the product
is), so the site effectively asks visitors to navigate several incompatible
category systems simultaneously, which he argues drives confusion and drop-
off, particularly for people who don't already know Razorpay's product
naming.

## Competitive analysis

The author reviewed Zendesk, Freshworks, and Stripe and found they all favor
a top-down pattern — show a small number of high-level categories first,
then reveal specific products once a visitor picks one — and contrasts this
with Razorpay's site, which puts many individual products on display at
once across multiple categorization schemes without that initial funnel.

## Solution: Razorpay Discovery

Rather than rearchitecting the whole site, the author proposes an additive
"logic form" — a short guided questionnaire (business size, the service
needed, transaction type, technical requirements) that filters down to
relevant products for the visitor, surfaced via the main navigation, the
homepage hero, and a side-sheet available during normal product browsing.
He gives it its own sub-brand (an atom-shaped mark) matching Razorpay's
existing visual language, designs it with chip-style multiple-choice
selections, and separately covers mobile layout and SEO considerations
(keeping existing indexed product pages intact rather than replacing them).

## Reflection and proposed metrics

The author is explicit that none of this was tested with real users or real
traffic — he proposes, rather than reports, success metrics an actual launch
should track: time spent in the flow, click-through rate out of the form,
and where in the flow people drop off. He frames this explicitly as a
hypothesis the design would need to earn through A/B testing, not a result.

## Closing

The piece ends with a short personal note, a recap connecting the proposed
solution back to the stated problem, suggested future directions, and
standard portfolio-style links to the author's other work and contact
details.
