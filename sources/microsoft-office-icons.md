## Opening

Friedman opens with a self-deprecating line about redesigns being more
intimidating than a blank page, then grounds the "why now": the icons hadn't
changed since 2013 (anchored with a cultural marker — selfies were still
Oxford's word of the year), while in that time Office itself changed hugely —
real-time collaborative tooling, AI features (data insights, voice-driven
writing, LinkedIn-informed resumes), and entirely new apps like Microsoft
Teams. He frames the new icons as a visible signal to customers that the
underlying product has genuinely changed.

## Heritage and future

The team explicitly built on Office's existing visual history rather than
starting over: the brand's traditionally strong colors were kept but shifted
to a palette the team describes as bolder, lighter, and friendlier. Gestalt
principles were used deliberately — each icon keeps a distinct, identifiable
symbol, but the symbols share enough visual language to read as one coherent
suite rather than unrelated apps.

## Designing across platforms, devices, and generations

Friedman names the real constraint directly: five generations of users work
across many platforms and devices and contexts (office, home, on the go), so
the visual language had to emotionally land across all of them. The core
decision: decouple the app's letter from its symbol, as two panels that can
sit together or apart depending on context, preserving a familiar anchor
(the letter) while letting the symbol carry simplicity on its own. This also
opened room for depth/3D treatments later, since the two elements are no
longer locked into one flat composition.

## Content over tool-chrome

The redesign deliberately dropped the literal container metaphor — Word's
icon no longer shows a document outline, Excel's no longer shows a
spreadsheet grid outline. Instead they show lines of text (Word) and
individual cells (Excel), i.e. a representation of the content people
create rather than the tool's traditional frame. In the same spirit, the
ratio between letter and symbol was flipped from the old 2:1 (letter-
dominant) toward emphasizing the symbol, on the reasoning that the letter
represents the tool while the symbol represents what people actually make
with it.

## Closing

Friedman calls the new icons the result of "many iterations... plenty of
late nights and weekends," explicitly frames them as part of an ongoing
journey rather than a finished artifact, invites public comments, and closes
with calls to action: follow Microsoft Design's social channels, join the
Windows/Office Insider programs, or apply to Microsoft design careers.
