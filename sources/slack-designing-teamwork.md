## Opening

The piece frames the release as a significant update addressing complexity
that accumulated as Slack grew — the header alone had grown to more than a
dozen clickable areas and two separate search bars, and menus had become
unwieldy enough that even longtime power users missed features buried in
unintuitive places. It states upfront that the team deliberately chose not
to rely only on A/B testing or internal brainstorming, and instead brought
real customers into the design process directly.

## Assembling the team

A small cross-functional group (design, engineering, research, product)
started by aggressively stripping the interface down and rebuilding
piece by piece, with an explicit operating principle of limiting the number
of choices presented to users rather than adding more.

## Co-designing with customers

Around 100 users from Slack's "champion network," spanning many
organizations globally, were brought into a shared Slack channel to give
fast, informal feedback on prototypes as they evolved — described as
enabling iteration within days rather than weeks. Specific things this
process surfaced: member count, initially considered unnecessary visual
noise, turned out to matter a lot for users judging the right tone to use
("reading the room"); features placed in less prominent spots (like channel
sections) were still found and adopted by motivated users with little
explanation needed; and moving search and message history into the top
navigation bar — a real departure from the prior layout — actually matched
how people using Slack across large organizations already expected it to be
organized. The authors credit the shared-channel format itself (not just the
feedback content) as valuable, since participants could react to each
other's comments and see iterations land in near real time.

## Learning from beginners

Separately, the team ran benchmarking studies specifically with brand-new
Slack users completing core tasks (messaging, searching, finding channels)
on the old design versus the new one. This uncovered different problems than
the power-user feedback did: a floating compose button at the bottom of the
sidebar went completely unnoticed on desktop until moved to the top; right-
clicking turned out to be an immediate, natural action for many new users
rather than a "power user" behavior, which led the team to expand right-
click functionality more broadly; and sections the team had collapsed by
default for a cleaner look (like the people directory and saved messages)
actually needed to start open, since new users weren't discovering they
existed otherwise. A second round of usability testing after these fixes
showed the new design matching or beating the old one on these tasks.

## Final polish

Smaller late-stage refinements included distinct animated feedback for
saving a message, platform-specific visual tweaks for Windows versus Mac,
visual consistency across "jump to" buttons, and new theme options that took
advantage of the new top bar layout for both minimal and more maximalist
visual preferences.

## Closing

The authors explicitly name the stakes of changing a tool this central —
citing roughly nine hours a day of connected time and around 90 minutes of
active use per user — and say that scale is exactly why they leaned on
direct customer collaboration rather than guessing internally. They
summarize their operating philosophy as favoring the obvious solution over
the clever one and not reinventing well-understood patterns, and frame the
release as a foundation for further changes rather than a finished redesign.
