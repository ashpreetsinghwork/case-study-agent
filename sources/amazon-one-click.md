## The problem framing

The piece opens on late-90s online checkout as genuinely tedious — sign up,
enter address, enter card number, click through several confirmation
screens — with many shoppers simply stopping partway through. It frames
Amazon's "Buy Now" (later "1-Click") not as a flashy launch but as a direct
response to that friction.

## Why it worked

The author's core claim is that this was a behavioral insight more than a
technical one: purchase intent is often a brief window of certainty, and
the longer checkout drags on, the more that certainty erodes into doubt,
tab-switching, and price comparison. So instead of adding more reassurance
steps (the industry norm), Amazon compressed checkout into a single
action — accepting the loss of an "are you sure?" confirmation as a
worthwhile trade for speed. The author cites commonly reported cart-
abandonment rates (~70%) as the ongoing cost of friction-heavy checkout that
1-Click was designed around.

## Effect on user behavior

The author argues 1-Click changed the *feel* of shopping on Amazon from a
deliberate "visit to a store" into something closer to habit — comparing it
to using a saved mental bookmark: you remember you need something, tap
once, and the rest happens without further attention. Prime's faster
delivery is framed as compounding this effect by shrinking the "waiting
period" that otherwise gives people time to reconsider a purchase.

## Industry adoption

The piece notes Amazon patented 1-Click in 1999, which forced competitors
either to license it or design workarounds, and that once the patent expired
in 2017 the same underlying idea (minimize the final purchase step)
proliferated everywhere — saved cards, autofill, express-pay buttons, and
is cited by the author as the shared logic behind Apple Pay, Google Pay, and
UPI-based apps.

## The generalized lesson

The author pulls back to a broader claim: successful digital products tend
to orient every supporting feature (search, reviews, delivery estimates,
recommendations) around one core action, without the supporting features
calling attention to themselves. From there the piece argues good UX is less
about visual polish and more about removing small irritations before they
compound into drop-off, and closes by generalizing the Amazon example into
advice for other teams: identify the one task a user actually came to do,
find what's slowing them down, and design everything else to support that,
explicitly framed as the agency's own consulting pitch rather than an
Amazon-sourced claim.
