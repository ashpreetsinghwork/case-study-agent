## Opening

The author opens on a personal, specific anecdote — getting a ride cancelled
while waiting outside in extreme cold during a "polar vortex" — using the
exact in-app cancellation message as a hook, then generalizes it: even after
requesting a ride, there's always some anxiety that pickup could go wrong,
and when it does, it usually just ends in a cancellation that wastes both
the rider's and driver's time.

## The problem

Framed as a single open question: how can drivers and riders find each other
more reliably? The author names specific, concrete failure modes rather than
a vague "pickup is hard" — a driver correctly at the building but on the
wrong side of it, a rider standing on the opposite side of the street from
where the app places them, and GPS unreliability generally worse in dense
cities. The piece acknowledges Uber already had some mitigations in place
before proposing more.

## Personas

Two named personas carry direct, quoted frustrations rather than the
author's own voice: a driver (Pete) who says the app's pin often sits in the
middle of the road with no indication of which side of the street to use,
and a rider (Justine) who describes manually dragging her pickup pin and
typing her address every time, only to still have drivers sent to the wrong
side of her building.

## Proposed solution

The core idea: let riders set a more precise pickup position using Google
Maps Street View (before arriving) or AR (once already at the location,
camera-enabled), save precise locations against Home/Work addresses for
repeat use, and let the driver see that precise pin while approaching. The
author frames the intended feeling as closer to waiting at a bus stop —
confident the vehicle will stop right in front of you — rather than the
current uncertainty, while keeping the feature fully optional for riders who
prefer the simpler default flow.

## Process

The author describes starting with paper sketches of the full end-to-end
flow, moving to high-fidelity screens in Sketch, and prototyping the key
interactions in After Effects. Screens shown cover both the rider placing a
precise pin before arriving, and the corresponding driver-side view — with
an explicit caveat that, lacking access to Uber's actual driver app, the
driver-side treatment is necessarily incomplete.

## Next steps and closing

The author proposes further user research with both drivers and riders,
deeper work on the driver-side app specifically, and usability testing to
find where the feature breaks down or goes undiscovered — framed as open
follow-up work rather than a finished solution. The piece ends with an
unrelated "bonus" concept sketch about reordering multi-stop trips.
